\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

We asked how the population used for behavioral fine-tuning shapes the predictions of a model intended to represent human cognition. Our results show that changing the fine-tuning population changes predictions on held-out items, even when the starting model, training budgets, and settings remain the same. Population composition therefore shapes what the model learns as typical human behavior. This poses a challenge for interpreting behavioral foundation models as general models of human cognition: their predictions may reflect the populations represented in their training data, and may not extend equally well to others.

We showed that there is an US prediction advantage already present in Llama 3.1, which increases in Centaur for some of the tasks. This raises concerns that behavioral fine-tuning may reinforce existing western-centric prediction biases. Increasing the amount of behavioral data alone may therefore be insufficient to improve generalizability. Which populations contribute to these datasets, and how they are balanced, also matters.

The transfer from the giving to the taking variant of social allocation shows that these effects can extend beyond the task variant used for fine-tuning, without preserving the meaning of the human population difference. The US adapter predicted both more tokens given and more tokens taken, although these responses have opposite meanings for generosity. This suggests that a numerical response tendency transferred more readily than its behavioral meaning. The adapters fine-tuned on the giving variant nevertheless improved predictive performance on the taking variant and outperformed response adjustment. Better scores therefore did not establish that the models captured the human population difference. That across tasks and populations, fine-tuning did not consistently outperform response adjustment, also highlights the need to test whether its predictive benefits go beyond changes in overall answer preferences.

  As LLMs move from being objects of psychological research to tools for generating scientific insights, biases in their behavioral training data may become biases in simulated findings. Our results show that whose data are included matters, while better predictive performance alone does not establish meaningful population representation. Before using these models in automated scientific discovery, their conclusions should be tested across populations and tasks and checked against new human data.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study used three datasets from two countries, relatively few items, and three random seeds. Risky-choice samples were particularly small, and the main transfer test involved closely related task variants. Country differences may also reflect language, recruitment, and demographics. Since we selected studies reporting cross-cultural differences, the findings do not establish how common these effects are across human cognition.

NLL also complicates comparisons between populations. A population can score better because participants choose the model's preferred answer more consistently, even when another population's response proportions match the model more closely. Lower NLL therefore does not necessarily indicate better representation of a population's response distribution. This concern mainly affects comparisons across populations; comparisons between models on the same responses share the underlying response variability.


\subsection{Future directions}

A cross-cultural dataset approaching the scale and breadth of Psych-101 would provide a stronger test of whether population composition produces a broadly ``WEIRD'' model. Training otherwise identical models on predominantly Western and more geographically balanced data would help establish whether the effects observed here accumulate across tasks and whether broader population coverage improves generalization.

The other biases discussed in the introduction offer related extensions. Publication bias could be tested by comparing training on studies reporting clear effects with a comparable collection that also includes null findings and replications. Task-selection bias could be examined by varying the balance of experimental paradigms and evaluating models on entire task families excluded from training.

Further work should also examine whether population-specific adaptation interferes with Centaur's existing capabilities. Freezing the original parameters does not guarantee that an active adapter preserves predictions on previously learned tasks. Broader retention tests, and comparisons with training on a mixture of original and new data, could help identify how to improve population coverage without losing earlier capabilities.
